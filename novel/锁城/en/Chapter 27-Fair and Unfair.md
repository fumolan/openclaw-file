# Chapter 27: Fair and Unfair

The first day after restoring fair mode, Quincy received seventeen complaints.

They all said the same thing: yesterday morning's queue was unfair. People who'd waited a long time had their spots taken by newer requests. Some had waited two days, only to be cut in front of by newcomers under unfair mode.

Quincy sat in the center of the tree canopy, reading each complaint one by one.

He didn't argue. He didn't apologize.

At the bottom of every complaint, he wrote a single line: "Extraordinary circumstances. Will not happen again."

Then he filed the complaints neatly and locked them in a copper box beneath the tree roots.

When Cole came to find him, he was polishing the copper box.

"Complaints?" Cole glanced at it.

"Seventeen." Quincy said. "Fewer than I expected. I thought at least fifty."

"Most people understand."

"Understanding isn't the same as accepting." Quincy placed the box back under the roots. "Fairness is the core of the rules. I broke the rules—even for just one day—and it shakes people's confidence."

"You made the right choice."

"The right choice isn't always the fair choice." Quincy said. "Unfair mode boosted queue throughput by forty percent. In that moment, efficiency mattered more than fairness. But—"

"But fairness is the whole reason the queue exists."

Quincy looked at him and nodded.

"Do you know what unfair mode really is?" Quincy asked.

"Cutting in line."

"More than that." Quincy said. "The core logic of unfair mode is—a new request can try to acquire a resource directly, without checking whether anyone else is waiting. If the resource happens to be free, the new request gets it immediately. If not, the new request waits its turn."

"That sounds—fairly reasonable."

"In some scenarios, it is." Quincy said. "Unfair mode has higher throughput because it skips the overhead of queuing. In highly competitive environments, fair mode has to maintain queue order—first come, first served. But maintaining order itself takes time. Unfair mode skips that step—whoever arrives first, takes it; if they get it, they work; if not, they queue."

"The cost?"

"Starvation." Quincy said. "In extreme cases, if a request has bad luck—every time a resource is freed, another new request snatches it first—that unlucky request might wait forever. That's starvation. A person queues for three days, getting cut every day, never reaching their turn."

"You wouldn't let that happen."

"Of course not." Quincy said. "But I need rules to prevent it. In fair mode, the rule is first come, first served—simple, clear, unshakeable. In unfair mode—there are no rules. Luck decides everything."

"So yesterday—"

"Yesterday I broke the rules." Quincy said. "I chose efficiency over fairness. In that moment, it was the right choice. But if someone makes that choice every day—efficiency today, efficiency tomorrow—fairness becomes a luxury. Eventually, no one queues anymore. Everyone cuts."

Cole thought about it. "So you restored fair mode."

"It had to be restored." Quincy said. "Unfair mode is a blade. You can draw it in an emergency. But after, you sheathe it. A blade can't become the default."

He opened another box beneath the tree roots and took out a small copper token. Etched on its surface was a symbol—FIFO. First In, First Out.

"This is my token." Quincy said. "First come, first served. From the day I took over the queue mechanism, this token has hung on the tree."

He hung the copper token back on the roots.

It swayed gently in the breeze, producing a clear, ringing sound.

"Cole." Quincy said.

"Hmm?"

"You helped Lockhaven through a crisis. But after the crisis, what the city needs is order. Not heroes."

"I understand."

"Do you really?" Quincy looked at him. "Heroes can break rules to solve problems. But a city can't run on broken rules. A city needs rules—fair ones, stable ones, ones everyone can rely on."

"Like your queue."

"Exactly." Quincy said. "My queue isn't perfect. Sometimes it's slow. Sometimes it's inefficient. But it's fair. Every person in line knows—as long as they wait, their turn will come. That certainty matters more than efficiency."

Cole looked at the FIFO copper token. Sunlight filtered through gaps in the canopy, falling on the token and reflecting warm light.

"Quincy." he said.

"What?"

"If I come queue up again someday—"

"You wait wherever your number is." Quincy said. "No special treatment for being Cole."

Cole smiled. His first smile in days.

"Thanks."

"No need to thank me. Queuing is how it should be."

Cole turned and left the plaza. Behind him, people stood quietly in their respective queues. No resentment on their faces—yes, they'd been cut yesterday, but today the rules were back. Fairness was back.

They were willing to keep waiting.

Because they knew—Quincy's queue wouldn't let them down.

In the distance, the Heap District's commercial lights were growing brighter. The Old Generation's sky was shifting from gray to pale blue.

The cracks were healing.

The city was recovering.

And fairness—had never truly left. It had just rested for a moment.
