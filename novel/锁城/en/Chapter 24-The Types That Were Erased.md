# Chapter 24: The Types That Were Erased

Meta was waiting at the bottom level of the Attic.

It was Cole's first time on this floor. The spiral staircase ended, and the world transformed completely. The upper levels had been neat and orderly—type definitions arranged by category like a vast library.

The bottom level was no library. It was chaos.

Countless data cables jutted from the walls, ceiling, and floor, tangled together in a massive knot. The end of each cable connected to a translucent panel, and on each panel was etched a type definition. Some panels were ancient, their surfaces scarred by time. Others were newer, their edges still bearing the marks of incremental updates.

Incremental update marks—those were what Donovan had planted over three years.

Meta stood at the center of the chaos. Her robes drifted among the data cables like a silver-gray flower.

"The token." She held out her hand.

Cole placed the silver badge in her palm. The token was already cooler than before—Donovan's residual identity data was fading fast.

Meta pressed the token against a panel. It glowed.

"Platform-level token—valid. But time is short. The residual identity data can sustain for—" she glanced at the token's readout, "fifteen minutes."

"Is that enough?"

"No." Meta said. "There are still over three hundred incremental updates remaining. A normal rollback takes thirty seconds each. Three hundred would take an hour and a half."

"Speed it up."

"I'm already running concurrent cleanup mode. Eight cleanup threads operating simultaneously. But the incremental updates have dependency chains—some incrementals are built on top of others. They must be rolled back in a specific order, otherwise—"

"Cascading failure."

"Correct." Meta's fingers flew across the panel. "But I can optimize the rollback sequence. If I map the dependency graph and find the critical path—rollback the base incrementals first, then the dependent ones—we can reduce the wait time."

"Can you do it?"

Meta gave him a look. In those cold eyes, Cole saw exhaustion for the first time.

"I've been the Attic's guardian for ten years," she said. "Maintaining the integrity of type definitions is my duty. But I've never rolled back this many incrementals at once."

"Then do something you've never done."

Meta didn't respond. She closed her eyes and spread her hands across the panel.

Eight cleanup threads launched simultaneously.

Cole stood beside her, watching the data shift on the panel. Each rolled-back incremental update vanished like chalk being erased—wiped clean, revealing the original type definition beneath.

The original type definitions were pristine—written when Lockhaven was founded, untampered with.

"Thirty rolled back." Meta's voice was calm but rapid. "Dependency graph—layer two begins."

The data cables on the panel were rearranging themselves. Some snapped—the incrementals they depended on had already been removed. The broken cables retracted to their original positions.

"Eighty rolled back. Layer three—dependencies getting complex."

Cole watched the tangled cables. The bottom level was slowly becoming orderly—chaos receding inch by inch. But the pace was still too slow.

"How much time left on the token?" he asked.

"Nine minutes."

"How many can we roll back in nine minutes?"

"At current speed—about two hundred. One hundred won't make it."

"What happens to the ones that don't get rolled back?"

"The remaining hundred incrementals will become orphan data," Meta said. "After the token expires, incrementals without a valid permission source will be purged by the Attic's base level—but not through rollback. Forced deletion."

"What's the difference between forced deletion and rollback?"

"Rollback is safe—restores incrementally in dependency order. Forced deletion is brute force—rips out incrementals regardless of dependencies. When some are deleted, others that depend on them will break."

"How badly?"

"Uncertain. Maybe minor. Or—"

"Cascading failure."

Meta nodded once.

Cole thought for three seconds.

"Roll back the most critical batch first," he said. "Which incrementals are foundational—depended on by the most others?"

Meta checked the dependency graph. "About twenty base incrementals. They're depended on by over two hundred others."

"Focus all efforts on those twenty."

"And then?"

"Then let the token expire. The remaining incrementals become orphan data and get force-deleted by the Attic. But because the twenty base incrementals have been safely rolled back, the dependent ones won't cause severe cascading when deleted—the foundation is solid. Whatever happens above won't affect what's below."

Meta looked at him. "You're saying—give up safe rollback for the upper layer and just protect the base?"

"Yes. Secure the base, let the rest be force-deleted. We lose some non-core type definitions, but Lockhaven's infrastructure stays intact."

Meta was silent for two seconds.

"You've made your decision."

"Do you agree?"

"I don't have a better plan." Meta said. "Base incrementals priority rollback—beginning."

Her fingers reprogrammed the cleanup sequence on the panel. Eight cleanup threads concentrated their fire on the twenty base incrementals.

Each base incremental rollback was complex—they involved modifications to foundational type definitions, pulling one thread that moved everything. But Meta's operations were precise and decisive. After each rollback, she immediately verified the integrity of the underlying type definition.

"Fifteen." she said. "Sixteen. Seventeen—"

The token's temperature was plummeting.

"Eighteen. Nineteen—"

Cracks appeared on the token's surface. Donovan's residual identity data was almost gone.

"Twenty!"

Meta pressed the rollback button for the twentieth base incremental at the last possible moment.

The token shattered.

Silver fragments scattered from her hand like falling stars.

The token was completely spent. Donovan's identity data had fully dissipated.

"The token—" Meta said. "It's gone."

"The remaining incrementals?"

"They've become orphan data. The Attic's base level is initiating forced deletion."

The data on the panels began shifting violently. The incrementals that had lost their permission source were tagged "orphan" one by one, then brutally deleted.

The deletion wasn't orderly like rollback—it was more like a flood. Some deletions triggered chain reactions, taking dependent incrementals with them. Some were clean; others left residue behind.

"Damage assessment." Meta studied the panel data. "Of the three hundred-plus incremental updates, twenty base incrementals were safely rolled back, one hundred eighty were auto-deleted. The remaining hundred or so had portions of non-core type definitions collateral-damaged during cascade deletion."

"Scope of impact?"

"Primarily affects Segment Three's data structures—that's where the incremental updates were concentrated. Type definitions in other areas are largely intact."

"Segment Three—was already destroyed."

"So the loss is acceptable."

Cole exhaled.

"Meta," he said. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me." Meta's voice returned to its usual cool detachment. "Go check on the Cleaner's progress. I still have cleanup work here."

Cole nodded once and turned toward the spiral staircase.

Behind him, Meta stood alone at the center of the bottom level. Data cables were rearranging themselves, panels gradually returning to order.

Chaos was receding.

But she knew some things would never return to the way they were.

A hundred type definitions, violently deleted, would not come back. They had once been part of Lockhaven—tampered with and injected, yes, but during their three years of existence, some thread-people and some objects had formed relationships with them.

Those relationships were severed.

Just like when Donovan disappeared—silent, without a trace.

Meta looked down and picked up a fragment of the token.

It still held a trace of warmth.

She slipped it into her pocket.

Then she went back to work.
