# Chapter 1 — The Pause at Three in the Morning

Three-seventeen in the morning.

Cole had just returned from patrolling the eastern section of the Heap District commercial belt and was about to rest for a bit at his place in Stack Alley. Stack Alley was the quietest part of Lockhaven — each lane belonged to a single thread folk. No one else could get in, and no one else would bother. He hung his biased lock on the door latch. The lock recognized his signature instantly and settled into place without a sound.

A biased lock was the lightest kind there was. As long as only one person used it, you barely noticed it existed. Cole never felt the need for anything heavier.

He'd barely sat down in his chair, feet still on the floor, when the entire city stopped.

No sound. No warning. No sign whatsoever.

One second, the distant lights of the Heap District commercial belt were still flickering, the air still humming with the vibration of flowing data. The next second, everything froze.

Cole's body was locked into the chair in that instant. Not a physical lock — something deeper. His limbs wouldn't move. His eyes could shift, but only across the narrow slice of world directly in front of him.

A pause.

A city-wide pause.

He knew what this was. Every thread folk did. The Sweepers were out.

Through the narrow window of Stack Alley, he could see the sky over the Heap District turn gray. Not clouds — the Sweepers' marking ripples. The ripples expanded outward from the lowest layer of the Heap, sweeping through level by level, like a giant eraser wiping across the city.

The first time the marking ripple passed over him, Cole saw a young thread folk. The young man was standing at the entrance of a passage in the commercial belt, holding a package he'd just retrieved from the queue.

When the ripple swept over him, his body began to turn transparent.

*No.* Cole wanted to scream, but his mouth wouldn't move either.

He watched helplessly as the young man's outline gradually faded. The young man's lips seemed to move one last time before he vanished — maybe "help me," maybe "no," maybe just a meaningless syllable.

Then the spot was empty. Nothing remained.

Cole closed his eyes for a moment. When he opened them again, the marking ripple had swept through more territory. In the distance, three or four thread folk were becoming transparent at the same time, vanishing simultaneously.

The Sweepers weren't killers — at least, they didn't think of themselves that way. They were simply reclaiming objects that no longer had any references. If a thread folk had lost all reference relationships — if no living thread folk had any connection to them — the Sweepers would deem them collectable.

The problem was, during a pause, you couldn't prove you still had references.

Because everyone was frozen.

Cole didn't know who that young man was. Maybe he truly had no references left. Maybe his reference chain had broken just before the pause. But maybe not. Maybe he still had references, but in the frozen state, they couldn't be displayed.

This was already the third pause this month.

Last month there'd been one. The month before that, none.

Cole silently noted the time — three-seventeen — and began waiting for it to end.

How long the pause lasted, he didn't know. There was no sense of time in the frozen state. Maybe a few seconds, maybe a few minutes. When the constraint suddenly released, Cole lurched forward, nearly falling out of his chair.

He was on his feet in an instant, pushing the door open.

Outside Stack Alley, a few thread folk were emerging from their lanes, wearing the dazed, frightened expressions of people just released from a pause. A middle-aged woman crouched on the ground, retching — some people had that physical reaction after coming out of a freeze.

"How long?" Cole asked a man nearby.

"I don't know. Felt like a long time." The man's voice was shaking. "Longer than last time."

Cole said nothing. He strode toward the Heap District commercial belt.

The scene there was worse than he'd expected.

Many spots were empty. Not just one or two — entire sections. Walking through a passage, Cole found three stalls on his left completely vacant, goods scattered on the ground, the vendors gone. Further away, on what should have been a busy supply line, seven or eight nodes had all stopped, with half a package still dangling from the end of the conveyor.

"Cole."

A voice came from behind. Cole turned and saw a tall, lean man leaning against the passage wall, arms crossed, a hint of a smile on his lips. Jace.

"Did you see that young one?" Jace asked. His tone was casual, as if he were asking about the weather.

Cole nodded.

"I counted," Jace stretched languidly. "This pause, eleven people vanished from the commercial belt. Last time it was four. The time before that — zero."

"You're tracking the disappearances?"

"Of course." Jace pulled a small notebook from his pocket and flipped it open for Cole to see. Dense notes recorded the time, duration, and disappearance count of every pause. Sloppy handwriting, but the data was clear.

Cole scanned the pages, his expression darkening.

The numbers were accelerating. Not just the disappearances — the frequency of pauses was increasing too. Three months ago, once every two months. Last month, once. This month, already three times.

"This isn't normal," Cole said.

"Of course it's not." Jace snapped the notebook shut. "You know what's the most abnormal part?"

"What?"

"I checked on the people who vanished." Jace's expression turned serious for the first time. "They weren't unreferenced. Cole, they had references. Their reference chains were intact."

Cole stopped walking.

Thread folk with intact reference chains should not be collected. That was the most fundamental rule of Lockhaven. The Sweepers could only reclaim objects unreachable from all GC Roots — if a thread folk still had living reference relationships, the Sweepers had no right to touch them.

"You're sure?"

"I personally traced three of their reference chains." Jace's voice dropped low. "Chains intact. But the Sweepers collected them anyway."

Cole was silent for a long time.

"Either the reference chain tracing mechanism is broken," he said slowly, "or the Sweepers' judgment criteria have been altered."

"Or," Jace looked at him, "someone tampered with things during the pause."

The pause at three-seventeen had ended, but the real investigation was just beginning.

Cole and Jace walked side by side down the empty passages of the commercial belt. The pre-dawn wind poured through gaps high up in the Heap, bitingly cold. Behind them, the vacant stalls cast long shadows in the early light.

"I need to see Donovan," Cole said.

"Donovan?" Jace raised an eyebrow. "You think he did it?"

"I don't think anything. It's his commercial zone where it happened. Every person who vanished was in his territory."

Jace whistled.

"Then let's go. But Cole —"

"Yeah?"

"If Donovan really is behind this, do you think his commercial zone is still safe?"

Cole didn't answer. He just pulled the biased lock from his pocket and gripped it in his palm.

The cool touch of the lock reassured him slightly. In Lockhaven, there was nothing a lock couldn't solve. At least, that's what he'd always believed.

But what happened at three-seventeen this morning gave him his first doubt.

In the distance, in the direction of the old generation, the sky still held traces of gray ripples. The Sweepers had retreated, but the marks remained. Those ripples were like scars etched across Lockhaven's canopy.

Cole looked up at them.

The ripples weren't fading.

They were spreading.
